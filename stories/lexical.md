# Stories: Lexical Rules

> **See also:** [`spec/lexical.md`](../spec/lexical.md) — the rules these chapters explain.

## Casing carries the kind

Every identifier in Zane answers one question before it answers any other: is this a type or a value? The [foundations story](foundations.md#the-compile-time-model-staged-types-cased-names) covers why encoding that answer in the source at all serves the captured-intent bet, and weighs it against the encodings Zane could have used instead — sigils on every use, or inferring an identifier's kind from where it was declared. That argument is the language-wide one, and it is not repeated here. What belongs to the lexical layer is narrower and more concrete: why the encoding is *case*, and what enforcing it costs and buys.

The encoding was noticed more than invented. Types are written capitalised already — `Int`, `Vector`, `Node` — by a convention so common it is nearly universal, and once that is granted the question inverts: if a type starts with a capital anyway, why permit the opposite at all? OCaml supplied the technique rather than the mapping. Its lexer splits capitalised identifiers from lowercase ones and its grammar leans on the divide — constructors and modules on one side, values and type names on the other — and the transferable idea was not *which* class each case denotes, which Zane sets its own way, but that the lexer can settle an identifier's class from its first letter and hand every later stage the answer. So Zane made the convention a rule: an uppercase-initial and a lowercase-initial identifier are different lexical classes — the parser's `UIDENT` and `LIDENT` — and everything downstream reads a name's kind off which one it is.

And the rule is total, a hard error rather than a lint that could be waived. A lowercase name in a type position is rejected outright ([`lexical.md` §3.1](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/b5f8af74ed636926ad75c3e6618c785112e137fb/spec/lexical.md#31-types-must-be-uppercase)); there is no path on which the compiler shrugs and guesses. This matters because the parser and every later reader lean on casing as ground truth — a rule that held only most of the time would be a rule neither could build on, the same reason the strictness elsewhere in the language forbids rather than merely discourages. Casing pays off only if it is total.

What it buys, at this layer, is that `<>` can mean type application at all. The angle brackets are also the comparison operators, and the two are told apart with no lookahead and no symbol table: the operand to the left of a `<` is a type exactly when it is uppercase, so `Vector<Int>` opens a type-argument list and `a < b` is a comparison, decided by a single token's first letter ([`lexical.md` §5](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/b5f8af74ed636926ad75c3e6618c785112e137fb/spec/lexical.md#5-how-casing-disambiguates-the-grammar)). A grammar that had to know what `a` and `Vector` *were* — by remembering their declarations — could not make that call while scanning; casing lets it. The cost is the naming latitude every capitalised-constant or lowercase-type-alias convention from other languages spends freely and Zane cannot; that trade is the foundations story's to weigh, and it does.

## Privacy lives in the name

Casing put a name's *kind* into the name itself, legible at every use site without chasing a declaration. When we came to field privacy, the question had the same shape — where should the fact that a field is private be written? — and it got the same answer, for the same reason.

Two decisions were tangled here, and it helps to take them in turn. The first was *how* privacy is spelled. Languages reach it three ways: a specifier per field (C#'s `private` on each member), a specifier over a range (C++'s `private:` heading a block), or the name itself (Dart's leading underscore). We ruled out the per-field specifier first: it clutters every member with a modifier, and while it is unarguably explicit, explicitness is not its exclusive property — a convention can be just as unambiguous without the noise. C++'s range form we actually liked; it is terse and it groups. But the thing that settled it for the name-based form is a virtue neither specifier form has: the marker rides along to the *use site*. A reader who meets `_id` in an expression sees that it is private right there, with no jump back to the declaration to find out; the specifier forms announce visibility only where the field is defined, and everywhere it is *used* the information is gone. Casing had already committed the language to putting a name's meaning where the name is read, not only where it is written, and privacy is that same idea applied to a different property.

The name-based form wins on a second count too, and it is the one we weigh most heavily: it is the simplest and most minimal of the three. It adds no grammar at all — no `private` keyword, no `private:` section, no per-member modifier slot — because it reuses the identifier the field already has. A property that costs zero new syntax and still reads at every use site is not a close call; the pull of the alternatives is mostly the gravity of the familiar.

So a leading `_` marks a field private ([`lexical.md` §4.2](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/b5f8af74ed636926ad75c3e6618c785112e137fb/spec/lexical.md#42-leading-underscore)), and it changes nothing about the name's lexical class — the first *letter* still fixes type-versus-value; the underscore is a separate signal layered on top.

The second decision was *what* privacy is private to, and here Zane's extensibility forced a question C++ never has to ask. The model we started from is C++'s: a private field is visible to the type's own methods and hidden from everyone else — privacy is a wall around the type, not around a file or a package. But in Zane methods are not sealed inside the type's declaration; they are package-scope verbs, and a method taking a `this` receiver for some type may be declared in *any* package, including one the type's author never saw. So "the type's own methods" no longer names a closed set. We had to decide whether a `this`-receiver method declared in a foreign package sees the private fields, and we said yes: privacy in Zane is the line between a method and a non-method, not between one package and another ([`types.md` §2.3](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/b5f8af74ed636926ad75c3e6618c785112e137fb/spec/types.md#23-field-visibility-is-name-based)). Any method whose first parameter is `this` for the type gets in, wherever it lives. We chose the general rule over the guarded one because generality is a bet the whole language makes — a rule with fewer special cases is easier to hold in the head and harder to trip over — and drawing the privacy line at the package boundary would have carved a special case into a model that did not otherwise need one.

The cost is a single, honest one, and it lands in tooling rather than in the language. Because visibility is spelled *in the name*, flipping a field from private to public is a rename, and a rename touches every site that mentions the field, where a specifier-based language flips one token in one place. By hand that is tedious; the real answer is a rename refactor, which is to say the cost is a dependence on a language server that does not exist yet and one day will. Beyond that the decision costs essentially nothing — no runtime, no grammar, no reader confusion — and pretending otherwise to look even-handed would be dishonest.

## A delimiter for each separated thing

Casing and the underscore load meaning onto the identifier; the last lexical decisions load it onto the punctuation *between* identifiers, under one governing rule — no mark may mean two things in the same context, so the parser never needs lookahead to know what a separator is separating.

The rule we settled on is that the delimiter is chosen by *what is being separated* and the bracket by *what kind of thing is inside* ([`lexical.md` §6](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/b5f8af74ed636926ad75c3e6618c785112e137fb/spec/lexical.md#6-delimiters-and-brackets)). A `;` terminates every member of a `struct` or `variant` body; a `,` separates the elements of a value collection; a newline separates statements; `{ }` encloses a member body or a code block; `[ ]` encloses a flat list. Each character has exactly one job in exactly one setting, so meeting it is unambiguous.

> [!NOTE]
> Superseded: the delimiter is no longer chosen by what is being separated, and a newline no longer separates statements. See "[The bracket picks the separator](#the-bracket-picks-the-separator)" and "[What had to be true before a brace could end a statement](#what-had-to-be-true-before-a-brace-could-end-a-statement)".

Two of these carry a deliberate asymmetry worth naming. The `;` is *always trailing* — every member ends in one, single-member or many, inline or spread over lines — and the `,` is *never trailing* — it appears only between elements, never after the last. That was a choice against the more common style where a body's last member may drop or optionally keep its separator. Making the terminator unconditional means newlines can be insignificant inside a body without a special rule for the final line, and making the two characters disagree on trailing behaviour keeps their roles visually distinct: a `;` seen after every member reads as *termination*, a `,` seen only between elements reads as *separation*. The bracket split does the same work one level up — because `{ }` always wraps a member body or code block and `[ ]` always wraps a flat list, the bracket alone tells the parser whether the newlines inside it are structural.

The honest cost here is small, and it is worth saying so plainly rather than inventing a drawback. The one thing a newcomer stumbles on is the always-trailing `;`: even inline, a body terminates its last member with a semicolon just like the rest — `struct { r Int; g Int; b Int; }` — which looks redundant next to languages that drop the final one. We take the uniformity, one rule with no exception for the final member, over saving a character, because the exception is what would have cost a reader more than the semicolon does.

## The bracket picks the separator

The chapter above states the rule as *the delimiter is chosen by what is being separated* — a member, a value-collection element, a statement. That reads like a principle, and we took it for one. It was not. It was a description of a list, and the list was what we were actually carrying around.

What exposed it was reaching for dynamic containers. Deciding what a map literal's entries should take meant stating the rule precisely enough to apply it to something the language did not yet have, and at that point the axis came apart in our hands. An `enum` body is a declaration — `type Colors = enum [ red, green, blue ]` — and it takes `[ ]` with `,`. "Declaration member versus value-collection element" was therefore already false about a construct we had been writing from the start. The obvious repair, that `;` goes wherever an entry pairs a name with a type, survived about a minute: a parameter list (`Node(id Int, scale Float)`), a generic header (`Buffer<T Type, n Number>`) and a field-constructor header (`Vector{x Int, y Int}`) all pair a name with a type, and all three take `,`.

What actually held was thinner than either candidate, and it ran only one way: a `;` appeared solely inside `{ }`, but a `{ }` did not imply a `;`, because `init{ }` and the field constructors took `,`. A one-directional rule cannot be stated in a sentence, and ours had quietly degenerated into three memorized constructs — a `struct` body, a `variant` body, `match` arms. The clearest symptom was in the syntax reference, which had to carve out "a constructor-header-specific exception" to describe a form that was not an exception to anything except our own account of the rule.

So we made it run both ways by keying it on the bracket instead. A `{ }` body terminates each entry with `;`, always trailing; a `[ ]`, `( )` or `< >` list separates its entries with `,`, never trailing ([`lexical.md` §6](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/0f9b44c2785b5805eb7e122ea6784c8f181dc23b/spec/lexical.md#6-delimiters-and-brackets)). It is a very simple rule and it works almost always, which is the most we ask of it. The reason it lands so well is a fact about how the forms are actually written rather than anything deep about what they mean: a `{ }` is very often multiline, while a `( )` or a `[ ]` much less often is. Always-trailing is what a multiline body wants — a final entry that carries the same mark as every other, so adding one touches one line — and never-trailing is what reads best on the single-line lists. The bracket was already correlated with the shape of the thing; we just stopped pretending the correlation was semantic and named the bracket as the rule.

We had considered dropping `;` altogether and separating everything with `,`. One mark is simpler than two, and it would have deleted the trailing asymmetry along with it. We rejected it on how a multiline body reads: in a body, `;` is doing a genuinely different job from `,` — it is not separating one item from the next, it is terminating each item — and terminating reads more naturally down a column of members than separating does. A separator makes the last item a special case; a terminator makes every item alike. The other direction we looked at, keying on whether an entry declares a name or supplies a value, was worse than both: it puts `;` inside parameter lists (`Node(id Int; scale Float;)`), and a parameter list is call-shaped, so a terminator there fights the form.

The honest cost is small and worth naming anyway. This is a second delimiter to learn, and `;` does not look as light on the page as `, ` does — `init{x; y;}` is visually heavier than `init{x, y}`, and `init{ }` is the most-written form in the language. We do not think it is a burden. It is what orthogonality costs here, and it is the kind of weight that stops registering once the forms are familiar. The rule's one real limit is not the weight but the branch: a `{ }` has two readings, a body of `;`-terminated entries and a code block of newline-separated statements, so the bracket picks the separator everywhere except that it also has to pick which kind of `{ }` it is. Every `{ }` is introduced by a token that says which — a mould keyword, `match`, `init`, a type name, a verb's signature — so nothing is ambiguous, but "almost always" is the accurate claim and we would rather write that than overstate it ([`lexical.md` §6.4](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/0f9b44c2785b5805eb7e122ea6784c8f181dc23b/spec/lexical.md#64-brackets-and-their-separators)).

> [!NOTE]
> Superseded: not every `{ }` is introduced by a token that says which reading it takes — a map literal has none. See "[The brace with nothing in front of it](#the-brace-with-nothing-in-front-of-it)".

The glossary term went with the axis. It had been the *member-versus-value delimiter*, a name that encoded the very distinction we had just found did not hold, so keeping it would have preserved the wrong idea in the one place a reader goes to look a term up. It is the **bracket-picked separator** now. The name is plain and it states what does the picking, which is the whole content of the rule; there was no rival candidate worth weighing, because once the bracket is what decides, naming anything else would be describing the old axis again.

## The brace with nothing in front of it

The chapter above rewrote the rule in order to reach dynamic containers, and then closed by reassuring itself about the one place the rule branches. A `{ }` has two readings — a body of `;`-terminated entries, and a code block of newline-separated statements — and the reassurance was that *every `{ }` is introduced by a token that says which*: a mould keyword, `match`, `init`, a type name, a verb's signature. That was true when it was written, and it stopped being true almost immediately, because the construct we had rewritten the rule *for* turned out to be the counterexample.

A map literal could have carried an introducing token. The obvious spelling is `Map{ ... }`, and it would have preserved the reassurance word for word. Two things ruled it out. The smaller one is that the slot in front of the brace is already taken: `Type{ ... }` is a field-constructor call, so `Map{ ... }` would be a call to a constructor named `Map`, and a reader meeting one would have no way to tell which they were looking at. The larger one is that it would be a lie about what the form is. A map literal is not a `Map`; it is a piece of syntax that any keyed type can consume, and it carries `@concepts$Map<K, V>` — a compiler concept, with no storage form and no claim to be a complete type ([`syntax.md` §2.8](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/6e300969fcbd999a6d973c0eefece222c7f2f566/spec/syntax.md#28-compiler-concept-types-for-literals)). Writing a type's name in front of it would put a complete type where the whole point is that there isn't one yet, in exactly the way `[ ]` does not name one either.

So the map literal stands alone in a value position with no token in front of it, and the two readings of `{ }` genuinely compete. The collision is not hypothetical: a block argument is also a braced thing in argument position, and it need not trail — `f({ x }, { y })` passes two of them ([`syntax.md` §4.9](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/6e300969fcbd999a6d973c0eefece222c7f2f566/spec/syntax.md#49-block-arguments)). We looked first at closing the collision from that side, by restricting block arguments to the trailing position only, which would have left the interior of an argument list unambiguously map territory. It was a real option and we did not take it, because it pays for a local ambiguity with a permanent restriction on an unrelated feature, and because a better answer turned out to be available at no cost at all.

The better answer is that the two readings are already distinguishable by shape. A body holds `;`-terminated entries; a code block holds statements. Nothing about that test needs a leading token — it needs only that the two shapes never coincide. And they coincide in exactly one case: the empty brace. `{}` is an empty block and would equally be an empty map, and no amount of looking at the entries settles it, because there are none.

> [!NOTE]
> Superseded: a body and a code block are no longer distinguishable by shape, since statements now end in `;` too. See "[What had to be true before a brace could end a statement](#what-had-to-be-true-before-a-brace-could-end-a-statement)".

Which turned the discrimination problem into a question we had to answer anyway, from the other end: what type does an empty literal have? A `[ ]` with no elements fixes no element type, and a `{ }` with no entries fixes neither a key type nor a value type. There were three ways out. We could require an annotation inside the literal, so the form carries the type the elements do not supply — rejected for the same reason `Map{ ... }` was, since a literal's whole character is that it defers the type to whatever consumes it, and a form that must name its own type is a constructor call wearing a literal's brackets. We could mint `@concepts$EmptyList` and `@concepts$EmptyMap`, concept types that exist purely to be converted away — which is machinery bought to keep one spelling legal, and it would have left the empty brace ambiguous between an empty map and an empty block regardless. Or we could ban the empty literal outright.

We banned it. A `[ ]` literal must hold at least one element and a `{ }` literal at least one entry, and an empty container is built by naming its type, which supplies what the literal cannot — `Array(Int, 0)` today, and the same move on whichever dynamic containers arrive later, whether as a plain constructor or a named one. The reasoning is that the ban is not a restriction imposed to buy the discrimination; it is the honest form of a fact that was already true. An empty literal never had a type to give, and every proposal for rescuing it amounted to writing the type down somewhere else in the expression. Once it was written down, a constructor was the natural place for it. That the ban also makes the shape test **total** — with no empty case left, a bare `{}` is always a code block and every other `{ }` is settled by its entries ([`lexical.md` §6.4](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/6e300969fcbd999a6d973c0eefece222c7f2f566/spec/lexical.md#64-brackets-and-their-separators)) — is the thing we got for free, and it is what let the block-argument restriction go back on the shelf unused.

The cost is real and it is paid at the keyboard. `doSomething([])` is not a thing you may write, and there is no shortage of situations where passing an empty collection is the natural thing to want; you name the container's type at the call instead, and you write out something you would rather have had inferred. We think that is the correct trade, because the inference was never actually available — the empty literal did not know the type either, and the alternatives only moved the moment of naming around — but it is a form the language now makes impossible rather than merely more verbose, and it is the first thing a newcomer will try.

What we would say about the reassurance we retired, finally, is that the reassurance was doing less work than it looked like it was doing. "Every `{ }` is introduced by a token" is a claim about *lookahead* — it says the reading is settled before the brace opens. The shape test is a claim about *content*, and it is the stronger of the two, because it does not depend on an inventory of introducing tokens staying complete as the language grows. The map literal was the first construct to fall outside that inventory. Keying the discrimination on what is inside the brace means the next one will not need a new rule.

## The straggler the rule had already caught

Keying the separator on the bracket settled the map literal, and settling the map literal sent us back over what was already written. If the bracket decides, then every construct in the language is making a claim about what it is simply by which bracket it wears, and we had never audited those claims — we had only ever changed the rule underneath them. One construct did not survive the audit.

An enum map attached uniform data to an enum's members, and it was written `Colors.colorName String [ red = "Red", green = "Green" ]`. Under the old axis that looked unremarkable. Under the new one it is the only place in the language where a `name = value` body is spelled as a `[ ]` list. Everything else that pairs a name with something takes `{ }` and `;`: the members of a `struct` or `variant`, the arms of a `match`, the fields of an `init{ }`, the entries of a field-constructor header or call site, and now the entries of a map literal. Everything in `[ ]` is a flat run of bare elements: array elements, the members of an `enum` body, the selectors of a `match` case group, the parameter types of a function type. Once the two piles are set side by side the enum map is plainly in the wrong one, and it had been the whole time.

The comparison that makes it unarguable is the field-constructor call site. `Weapon{name = String("Pistol"); damage = Float(10);}` and `Colors.colorName String [ red = "Red", ... ]` are the same construct wearing different clothes: a header that states a type once, and a run of entries that each name a slot and supply a value for it. They had just been given the same treatment in the type rules, too — an enum-map entry became a coercion site on exactly the terms a field-constructor entry already was ([`types.md` §4.2](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/5cd44fe9227ce8db43458bd493c9ebe35d75478a/spec/types.md#42-coercion-sites)). Two forms that agree on what they mean and on how the compiler treats them should not disagree on their punctuation.

So the enum map takes the brace and the semicolon ([`adt.md` §6](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/5cd44fe9227ce8db43458bd493c9ebe35d75478a/spec/adt.md#6-enum-maps)), and the `enum` declaration it ranges over keeps its `[ ]`, because an `enum` body really is a flat list of bare members. The one argument for leaving things alone was that the two had looked like a matched pair on the page — `enum [ ... ]` above, `[ ... ]` below — and that pairing is what we gave up. We think the resemblance was misleading rather than useful: the declaration lists members, the map fills a slot per member, and the brackets now say which is which instead of implying the two are the same kind of thing. That is the rule doing its job, not an exception to it.

The audit paid for itself in one small, concrete way, which is worth recording because it is the sort of thing that usually goes unnoticed. The repository's guard against reintroduced commas cannot be extended to multi-line bodies, because an indented entry line ending in `,` is legitimate in constructs that keep their commas. There used to be two such constructs; the enum map was one of them. Now there is only the multi-line `enum` body. The guard did not get stronger, but the reason it has to stay weak got smaller, and a rule whose exceptions are shrinking is usually a rule that was right.

What we did not do is go back and correct the older chapters. [The chapter that introduced enum maps](adt.md#payloadless-peers-keep-their-data-outside) shows the `[ ]` form, and it stays that way: it was true when it was written, and the record of the language having once spelled it differently is worth more than a tidy file.

## What had to be true before a brace could end a statement

The bracket rule had been settled twice and audited once, and the thing that kept turning up was not the separator at all — it was the end of a statement. Trailing arguments made it unavoidable. A call that moves its last argument outside the parentheses elides the `)`, so the `}` of that argument is the last mark on the line; the question is whether the statement is over. We wanted it to be. A brace that closes the call and the statement at once is the whole appeal of the form — one mark doing the closing work that the parenthesized version needs two for.

We could not have it, and the obstacle was a rule written years earlier for a different reason. A newline separated statements; the spec called it the one place a newline was structural. While that held, a `}` could not end a statement, because the newline already did. Declaring that a brace ends one too would not have been a new rule so much as a second rule competing with the first over the same boundary, and the reader would have had to know which won. The layout owned the statement, and nothing else could.

So the semicolon went in to take the boundary away from the layout. That is the whole of its purpose, and it is worth being exact about it, because a terminator on every statement looks like an aesthetic choice and was not one. We did not add `;` because a column of statements reads better with one, or because two statements per line is something anyone wanted; those are consequences, and mild ones. We added it so that a statement would carry its own end, and once a statement carries its own end, saying that a `}` also ends one is an ordinary claim about the grammar rather than a fight with the whitespace. The `;` is scaffolding for the brace rule. It exists so that, in the place we cared about most, it does not have to be written.

Which is why the trailing form takes no terminator, and why the alternative felt wrong before we could say why. Writing it out —

```zane
if(true) {
    print("hi");
};
```

— the `;` reads as though it belongs to the block, and the desugaring shows that it does not. In full, the call is `if(true, { print("hi"); });`, and the `)` slides in between the `}` and the `;`: the terminator was never part of what got passed to the callee. A mark that consistently implies the wrong grouping is worse than no mark, and this one was redundant besides, since a trailing argument is required to be the last thing in its statement and the brace is therefore already unambiguous. Nothing may follow it ([`lexical.md` §6.3](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/e07c48d57291ea84af766a720f298c0431986ae5/spec/lexical.md#63--terminates-a-statement)).

Two other things fell out of the same move. The first is that a `{ }` may no longer open a statement at all ([`lexical.md` §6.3.1](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/e07c48d57291ea84af766a720f298c0431986ae5/spec/lexical.md#631-a---may-not-open-a-statement)); scoping a run of work is a call taking a block argument, `core`'s `do(block)`, which reads the same and costs no grammar. With raw blocks gone and `;` marking where statements end, the rule that a trailing `{` had to open on the same line as its call — written to keep a statement block on the next line from being swallowed — had nothing left to disambiguate, and went with it. Three rules retired for one added, which is the trade we were hoping for and do not always get.

The second is that trailing stopped being a property of blocks specifically. Any last argument can take the position if it is a `{ }` — a block or a map literal ([`syntax.md` §4.9](https://github.com/zane-lang/spec/blob/e07c48d57291ea84af766a720f298c0431986ae5/spec/syntax.md#49-block-arguments-and-trailing-arguments)) — because size is what earns the position and a map literal gets just as large. We tried to extend it to `[ ]` as well, on the same reasoning, and pulled back: `expr[args]` is already subscript syntax, so a trailing `[ ]` would make the reading of an opening `[` depend on whether a call preceded it. That is resolvable but it is exactly the context-dependence the bracket rule exists to abolish, and we had just spent two chapters removing that kind of thing. `{` has no such rival.

The cost lands on the chapter before last. [The brace with nothing in front of it](#the-brace-with-nothing-in-front-of-it) settled map-literal-versus-block by their contents: a body held `;`-terminated entries, a code block held statements, and the terminator told them apart at the level of tokens. Once statements carry `;` too, that test is gone — both now hold `;`-terminated things. What replaces it is a parse rather than a scan: read one expression, then look at the next mark, where a `,` opens a map entry's value and a `;` ends a statement. It still decides every case, including the one that looks like a counterexample, since a `match` consumes its own scrutinee commas before the entry's mark is reached. But the claim that the two are distinguished by shape was true when it was written and is not now, and a reader of that chapter should know the ground moved under it.

The honest price of the whole thing is more punctuation than before. Every statement in every body now ends in a mark that was not there, which is a real amount of ink for a language that had gone out of its way to need none. We think it buys more than it costs, because what it buys is not the semicolons — it is their absence at the one place a reader looks hardest, the end of a trailing block, where the brace now says everything.
